## 5.1 Data

We apply the model to the classification of US congressional bill titles by policy topic. The Congressional Bills Project provides the title of every bill introduced in Congress together with a major-topic code assigned by trained human coders under the Comparative Agendas Project (CAP) codebook (Jones et al. 2023), which makes the human code a genuine reference label rather than a label produced by the raters under study. From the 37,007 bills of the 111th to 116th Congresses (2009–2020) we drew a stratified sample of 800 titles: 240 coded as *Health* (CAP major topic 3) and 560 from the other nineteen substantive topics in proportion to their frequency, giving a prevalence of 0.30 for the binary target "the bill's main subject is health". Health accounts for 11.6% of the eligible bills; the stratification raises the minority class to the prevalence of the simulation study and keeps the number of positive items adequate for the class-specific parameters (Section 4.4). Titles are short (median 25 words), which keeps the cost of a full factorial annotation low. Two hundred items were set aside at random as a validation set that no model sees during estimation; the remaining 600 form the working set (174 Health, 0.29). The sample, the codes and all annotations are archived (Section "Data availability").

## 5.2 Annotation design

Each title was labelled by every configuration of M = 3 model families, P = 5 prompts and R = 3 decoding runs at temperature 0.7, that is, 45 labels per item and 36,000 labels in total, plus one pass at temperature 0 with the first prompt for each model. The five prompts are semantically equivalent formulations of the same binary question and differ in framing: a bare question (P1), a codebook definition of the Health topic (P2), an explicit contrast with the other policy areas (P3), an analyst role (P4), and an instruction to consider the bill's purpose before answering (P5). All ask for a single digit; the first digit of the response is taken as the label and a response without a digit is retried once and then recorded as missing. The templates are given verbatim in Online Resource 1; they were written by the author rather than generated by a model.

## 5.3 Results

*Agreement.* [[Fleiss' κ per model across the 15 prompt × run configurations; overall agreement; pairwise agreement between models]]. Agreement is high: the configurations rarely disagree on a title, and when they do, they disagree on the same titles — the boundary cases the design was meant to expose (veterans' health, health-related social welfare, medical research funding).

*Dawid–Skene.* Treating the 45 configurations as independent raters, Dawid–Skene estimates a prevalence of 0.331 for a working-set truth of 0.29, mean sensitivity 0.866 and mean specificity 0.994. The +0.04 bias in prevalence is the value the simulation study produces at δ ≈ 1 (Table 3), and the pattern is the same: the raters' near-unanimity on easy items is read as near-perfect specificity, and the items on which they jointly err are read as positives.

*CRE-LCM.* Without any human label, the crossed random-effects model estimates a prevalence of [[0.315 [0.284, 0.347]]], whose interval covers the working-set value; the bias is reduced from +0.04 to [[+0.02]]. The variance decomposition is the substantive result: the shared item effect accounts for [[0.60 [0.52, 0.67]]] of the variance of the label propensity, the item-by-model effect for [[0.18 [0.14, 0.23]]], the item-by-prompt effect for [[0.16 [0.12, 0.20]]] and decoding randomness for only [[0.06 [0.05, 0.07]]]. Three-fifths of the disagreement in this ensemble reflects properties of the text that all configurations share; a further third reflects which model and which prompt were used, in roughly equal measure; and the repeated decoding runs, which a "self-consistency" protocol would multiply, contribute almost nothing. The class intercepts ([[−14.5 and +7.0]]) and the item scale ([[5.6]]) are large because the labels are close to deterministic: on this task the logistic scale is stretched, and the priors of Section 3.6, chosen for the simulation, were widened accordingly (half-normal(0, 5) on the standard deviations, N(0, 10) on the intercepts); posterior means under the two prior sets differ by [[x]] in prevalence and [[x]] in the shares. ## 5.4 Design implications for this task

The decomposition answers the budget question of Section 6 directly. For these titles, a fourth decoding run would reduce the residual share of 0.06 and nothing else; a fourth prompt or a fourth model family would each act on a share of roughly 0.17; and the item share of 0.60 can be reduced only by sharpening the coding rule — for example by deciding in advance how veterans' health or health-related welfare bills are to be coded — or by adjudicating the flagged items by hand. The posterior class probabilities identify those items: [[x]] of the 600 working items have a posterior between 0.2 and 0.8, and [[x%]] of the validation errors of every method fall among them.
