Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to label text at scale, but every label is produced by a \emph{configuration}---a model, a prompt and a stochastic decoding run---and configurations that share training corpora and alignment procedures do not err independently. Classical aggregation models, from Dawid and Skene onward, assume conditional independence of raters given the true class. We propose a latent class model whose dependence structure follows the factorial design of the annotation ensemble: item-level random effects shared by all configurations, crossed with item-by-model and item-by-prompt effects, with decoding runs exchangeable. Estimated by Bayesian inference with the latent class marginalised and the shared item effect integrated by quadrature, the model recovers point identification without a gold standard and decomposes annotation error variance into item, model, prompt and run components. In a simulation study, Dawid--Skene increasingly overstates annotator accuracy and biases prevalence as dependence grows; the proposed model reduces the prevalence bias several-fold (from $+0.05$ and $+0.10$ to about $+0.01$ and $+0.04$ at moderate and strong dependence), restores nominal interval coverage and yields better-calibrated posterior class probabilities. In an application to policy-topic coding of 800 US congressional bills by three model families, five prompts and three runs, the ensemble is near-unanimous and all aggregators reach similar accuracy and calibration against human codes; the gain lies in the prevalence, whose bias is halved (from $+0.031$ to $+0.015$), and in the per-configuration accuracy estimates. The variance decomposition translates directly into design guidance for how many models, prompts and runs to use.
