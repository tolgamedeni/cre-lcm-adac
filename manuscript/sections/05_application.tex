\section{Application: policy-topic coding of US congressional bills}\label{sec:application}

\subsection{Data}\label{sec:app_data}

We apply the model to the classification of US congressional bill titles by policy topic. The
Congressional Bills Project \citep{Wilkerson2025} provides the title of every bill introduced in
Congress together with a major-topic code assigned by trained human coders under the Comparative
Agendas Project (CAP) codebook \citep{Jones2025}, which makes the human code a genuine reference
label rather than a label produced by the raters under study. From the 37,007 bills of the 111th
to 116th Congresses (2009--2020) we drew a stratified sample of 800 titles: 240 coded as
\emph{Health} (CAP major topic 3) and 560 from the other nineteen substantive topics in proportion
to their frequency, giving a prevalence of 0.30 for the binary target ``the bill's main subject
is health''. Health accounts for 11.6\% of the eligible bills; the stratification raises the
minority class to the prevalence of the simulation study and keeps the number of positive items
adequate for the class-specific parameters (Section~\ref{sec:sim_results}). Titles are short
(median 25 words), which keeps the cost of a full factorial annotation low. Two hundred items were
set aside at random as a validation set whose human codes are never used in estimation and which
is never anchored; the remaining 600 form the working set (174 Health, 0.29). The sample, the
codes and all annotations are archived (see the data availability statement).

\subsection{Annotation design}\label{sec:app_design}

Each title was labelled by every configuration of $M = 3$ model families, $P = 5$ prompts and
$R = 3$ decoding runs at temperature 0.7, that is, 45 labels per item and 36,000 labels in total,
plus one pass at temperature 0 with the first prompt for each model. The five prompts are
semantically equivalent formulations of the same binary question and differ in framing: a bare
question (P1), a codebook definition of the Health topic (P2), an explicit contrast with the
other policy areas (P3), an analyst role (P4), and an instruction to consider the bill's purpose
before answering (P5). All ask for a single digit; the first digit of the response is taken as
the label and a response without a digit is retried once and then recorded as missing. The
templates are given verbatim in Online Resource 1; they were written by the author rather than
generated by a model. Every call used a 16-token output budget, and every raw response is stored
with its timestamp, model version, prompt, run and temperature.

The three model families were chosen to span the design space that the method is meant to
describe rather than to maximise accuracy. First, each family comes from a different developer
with a different training corpus and alignment procedure (Anthropic, Google, Alibaba), so that the
item-by-model effect $\phi_{im}$ captures genuine between-family dependence rather than variation
among versions of one model. Second, the two commercial models are the efficiency tier of their
families (Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash), which is the tier practitioners use for annotation
at scale: \citet{Carlson2025} report that such models match or approach the larger tiers on binary
classification at a fraction of the cost, and cost is what makes the model $\times$ prompt
$\times$ run design affordable. Third, one family is an open-weights model run locally (Qwen 2.5
7B Instruct, 4-bit quantised, served by Ollama): its weights and digest are archived, so this arm
of the ensemble is exactly reproducible, which no API-served model can guarantee once its version
is retired \citep{Barrie2025}. Larger or reasoning-oriented models were deliberately excluded:
they raise cost by one to two orders of magnitude, their suitability for plain classification is
unclear \citep{Carlson2025}, and the question of the paper is not which model is best but how
dependent the members of an ensemble are. Model identifiers, quantisation, decoding parameters,
query dates, call counts, missing rates and cost are given in Table~\ref{tab:providers}; all raw
responses are archived.

Table~\ref{tab:providers} reports, per provider, the model identifier, quantisation, decoding
settings, query dates, number of calls, missing rate and cost. The three providers answered
12,800 calls each; Anthropic and the local model returned a digit in every case, Gemini in all but
10 (0.08\%). Total API cost was USD 3.31; the local model cost nothing. The ten missing labels are
treated as missing at random, which the binomial formulation accommodates through a reduced run
count $R_{imp}$ for the affected configuration--item pairs.

\subsection{Results}\label{sec:app_results}

\emph{Agreement.} Fleiss' $\kappa$ across the fifteen prompt $\times$ run configurations is 0.85
for Claude Haiku, 0.97 for Gemini Flash and 0.84 for Qwen, and 0.84 over all 45 configurations;
mean pairwise agreement is 0.94, 0.99 and 0.94 within the three models and 0.92 to 0.94 between
them (Table~\ref{tab:agreement}). Agreement is high: the configurations rarely disagree on a
title, and when they do, they disagree on the same titles---the boundary cases the design was
meant to expose. The 116 bills (14.5\%) that receive between 6 and 39 Health labels out of 45 are
mostly Medicare and Medicaid financing, veterans' health and drug-control bills: 72\% of them are
coded Health by CAP and the rest mainly law and crime, defence or social welfare.

\emph{Dawid--Skene.} Treating the 45 configurations as independent raters, Dawid--Skene
estimates a prevalence of 0.331 for a sample value of 0.300, mean sensitivity 0.866 and mean
specificity 0.994; its posterior class probabilities average 0.321 on the working set (CAP 0.291)
and 0.370 on the validation set (CAP 0.330). The +0.03 bias in prevalence lies between the values
the simulation study produces at $\delta = 0.5$ and $\delta = 1$ (Table~\ref{tab:prev}), and the
pattern is the same: the raters' near-unanimity on easy items is read as near-perfect
specificity, and the items on which they jointly err are read as positives.

\emph{CRE-LCM.} Without any human label, the crossed random-effects model estimates a prevalence
of 0.315 [0.284, 0.347], whose interval covers the sample value; the bias is halved, from +0.031
to +0.015. The variance decomposition is the substantive result (Table~\ref{tab:app_shares},
reported from the anchored fit, which converges; the unanchored point estimates agree within
0.01): the shared item effect accounts for 0.59 [0.53, 0.65] of the variance of the
label propensity, the item-by-model effect for 0.19 [0.15, 0.23], the item-by-prompt effect for
0.16 [0.13, 0.19] and decoding randomness for only 0.06 [0.05, 0.07]. Three-fifths of the
disagreement in this ensemble reflects properties of the text that all configurations share; a
further third reflects which model and which prompt were used, in roughly equal measure; and the
repeated decoding runs, which a ``self-consistency'' protocol would multiply, contribute almost
nothing. The class intercepts ($-14.5$ and $+7.1$) and the item scale (5.6) are large because the
labels are close to deterministic: on this task the logistic scale is stretched. We keep the
priors of Section~\ref{sec:estimation}, chosen for the simulation, as the primary specification
and report a refit with wider priors below. In the unanchored fit the item scale and the shares
mix slowly on this stretched scale (bulk ESS 8 to 16 from 6,000 draws, $\widehat{R}$ up to 1.46),
while the prevalence mixes well (ESS 1,160); all four chains sit at the same values and none shows
the merged-class mode.

\emph{Anchoring.} With 5\% of the working items (30) anchored to their CAP code, the prevalence
estimate is 0.311 [0.281, 0.343] and the shares are unchanged (0.59, 0.19, 0.16 and 0.06 against
0.60, 0.18, 0.16 and 0.06 without anchors); on the 570 non-anchored items the posterior labels
reach an accuracy of 0.960 and a Brier score of 0.038. The anchors matter here for mixing rather
than for the estimates: every parameter converges ($\widehat{R} \le 1.04$, bulk ESS of the shares
134 to 349).

\emph{Prior sensitivity.} Refitting both models with wider priors ($\mathrm{N}(0, 10)$ on the
intercepts, half-normal$(0, 5)$ on the three standard deviations and on $\tau_k$; four chains of
4,000 iterations) moves the latent scale but not the substance
(Table~\ref{tab:prior_sens}, Appendix~\ref{app:prior_sens}). With near-unanimous labels the
likelihood is nearly flat in the scale of the latent effects---the data are close to
quasi-complete separation---so under the wide priors the class intercepts drift to about $-22$
and $+11$, $\sigma_\theta$ to 7.8--8.5, the chains mix poorly ($\widehat{R}$ up to 1.9), and the
absolute shares follow the prior: the item share rises to 0.64--0.69 and the run share falls to
0.03--0.04. The ranking item $\gg$ model $\approx$ prompt $\gg$ run does not change, nor do the
prevalence (0.31--0.32), the accuracy and Brier score against the CAP codes (to within 0.002), the
per-configuration sensitivities and specificities (to within 0.025) and the posterior predictive
check.

\emph{Validation.} On the 200 held-out items, majority vote, Dawid--Skene and CRE-LCM classify
97.0\%, 96.0\% and 97.0\% correctly, with Brier scores of 0.030, 0.040 and 0.031; on the 570
non-anchored working items the figures are 95.4\%, 95.6\% and 96.0\% with Brier scores of 0.046,
0.044 and 0.039 (Table~\ref{tab:app_eval}). CRE-LCM's gain over Dawid--Skene is in calibration
rather than in accuracy, as in the simulation; against majority vote, whose hard labels make the
Brier score equal to the error rate, the gain appears only on the larger working set. The
posterior probabilities of both model-based methods are close to 0 or 1 for almost every bill, so
the calibration plot (Fig.~\ref{fig:calib}) is dominated by its two end bins.
Table~\ref{tab:app_percfg} compares, for each of the 15 model $\times$ prompt configurations, the
sensitivity and specificity implied by CRE-LCM with the values computed against the CAP codes of
all 800 bills: the model-based values track the observed ones within 0.03 on average (at most
0.08 for sensitivity and 0.04 for specificity), whereas Dawid--Skene overstates specificity by
0.02 to 0.05 (0.036 on average) and compresses its range across configurations to 0.021, against
0.043 in the CAP values. CRE-LCM narrows but does not remove the gap in specificity (+0.026 on
average, range 0.024), and the CAP value falls inside its 95\% interval for only 6 of the 15
sensitivities and 2 of the 15 specificities: bills that the ensemble labels Health but CAP codes
under another topic are, for any latent class model, positives with an ambiguous item rather than
errors of the raters.
The posterior predictive check (Fig.~\ref{fig:ppc}) compares the observed distribution of the
number of Health labels per item across the 45 configurations with its replicated distribution
under CRE-LCM and under Dawid--Skene: CRE-LCM reproduces the U-shaped distribution with its thin
but non-empty middle (5.6\% of bills with 15 to 30 Health labels, replicated 4.9\%; 20.4\% with 40
or more, replicated 20.6\%), whereas Dawid--Skene, whose raters are independent given the class,
predicts almost no intermediate counts (0.006\% with 15 to 30) and places the Health bills at 36
to 41 labels instead of 42 to 45 (13.8\% with 40 or more).

\emph{Temperature 0.} The temperature-0 pass with prompt 1 agrees with the temperature-0.7
majority for the same prompt on 99.9\%, 100\% and 98.5\% of items for Claude Haiku, Gemini Flash
and Qwen, with the same accuracy against the CAP codes (0.94, 0.96 and 0.92); the three models at
temperature 0 disagree with one another on 9.6\% of items, so a deterministic decoding setting
removes the smallest variance component and leaves the two largest untouched.

\emph{Shared construct bias.} All six validation errors of CRE-LCM are confident (posterior
probability of Health above 0.99) and point in the same direction: the ensemble labels as Health
bills that CAP coded under another topic---two drug-control bills (controlled-substance analogues,
medical marijuana), a mental-health screening of military recruits, a medical-isotope supply bill,
a restriction on research involving wards of the State and a fuel-tax refund for mobile
mammography vehicles. The per-configuration comparison shows the same pattern, with the CAP values
often outside the CRE-LCM intervals. Both reflect a difference between the LLMs' shared notion of
``health'' and the CAP coding rule, which assigns such bills to their legal or institutional home,
and the difference is common to every configuration. A latent class model cannot identify a bias
shared by all raters without a gold standard: the bias becomes part of the latent class, which here
is health as the LLMs read it rather than the CAP topic. Anchoring a small coded subset is the
remedy, because the anchors tie the latent class to the reference coding; but it works only if the
anchors include boundary bills. The 30 anchors drawn at random here contain one non-Health bill
with a substantial share of Health labels, and the anchored fit makes the same six validation
errors. This is the measurement counterpart of the reliability-without-validity finding of
\citet{Norman2026}: LLM raters agree with one another more closely than with task-specific human
coding, and no model of their agreement alone can recover the difference.

\subsection{Design implications for this task}\label{sec:app_design_implications}

The decomposition answers the budget question of Section~\ref{sec:discussion} directly. For these
titles, a fourth decoding run would reduce the residual share of 0.06 and nothing else; a fourth
prompt or a fourth model family would each act on a share of roughly 0.17; and the item share of
0.59 can be reduced only by sharpening the coding rule---for example by deciding in advance how
veterans' health or health-related welfare bills are to be coded---or by adjudicating the flagged
items by hand. The posterior class probabilities identify few such items: only 5 of the 600
working items have a posterior between 0.2 and 0.8, and none of the six validation errors of
CRE-LCM (one of the eight of Dawid--Skene) falls among the flagged items. The errors against the
CAP codes are the confident, shared ones described above. The agreement count is the better guide for
adjudication: four of the six errors are among the 32 validation bills with 6 to 39 Health labels
out of 45, although their Health posterior exceeds 0.8.
